Everything worth knowing before you buy solar

The basics

How a rooftop solar system actually works

There is no magic and there are no moving parts. Sunlight hits silicon, silicon produces electricity, and a box on your wall converts it into the kind your house can use.

Sunlight hits the panels

Each panel is a sheet of silicon cells under tempered glass. When light strikes silicon it knocks electrons loose — the photovoltaic effect. This produces direct current (DC) electricity. It works on cloudy days too, just at reduced output. Heat is not what generates power; light is. In fact panels lose a little efficiency as they get hotter.

The inverter converts DC to AC

Your home runs on alternating current (AC) at 230 V. The inverter converts the panels' DC into grid-quality AC, and it also constantly hunts for the point on the panel's curve that yields the most power — that's what "MPPT" on a spec sheet means. The inverter is the brain of the system and the component most likely to need service in 25 years.

Your appliances use it first

Solar power always feeds your own loads first. Fans, lights, fridge, AC, pump — whatever is running at that moment is served by the panels before a single unit is drawn from the grid.

Surplus flows to the grid

Around midday you'll generate more than you use. That surplus flows out through your bi-directional net meter, and the discom credits those units to your account. In effect the grid becomes a free battery — you deposit units at noon and withdraw them at night.

You're billed on the difference

At the end of the billing cycle you pay only for the net units drawn: what you imported minus what you exported. If you exported more than you imported, the credit usually carries forward to the next month.

A useful mental model: your panels are a tap, your house is a bucket, and the grid is a reservoir. When the tap fills the bucket faster than you empty it, the overflow goes to the reservoir and you get credit. At night you draw from the reservoir against that credit.
Components

What you're actually paying for

Panels are only about half the cost of a system. Any quotation that lists only the panel brand is hiding something — insist on seeing all six of these.

Solar panels (modules)

Roughly 45–55% of system cost. Check: brand, model, wattage, ALMM/DCR status, and both warranties (product and power output). Ask for serial numbers on delivery.

Inverter

Around 15% of cost. String inverters are standard for homes; microinverters cost more but handle shading better. Look for a 5–10 year warranty and check the make, not just "branded inverter".

Mounting structure

The part people ignore and later regret. Galvanised iron or aluminium, wind-rated for your region. A cheap structure rusts through in five years and takes the panels with it in a storm.

Cabling & protection

UV-resistant DC cable, correctly rated AC cable, DC and AC combiner boxes, surge protection devices and MCBs. Undersized cable silently wastes generation for 25 years.

Earthing & lightning arrestor

Mandatory, not optional. Proper earthing pits and a lightning arrestor protect both your system and your home. This is the first thing cut from a suspiciously cheap quotation.

Net meter & monitoring

The bi-directional meter is installed by your discom. Most modern inverters also offer app-based monitoring so you can see daily generation and spot a problem early.

Choosing a system

On-grid, off-grid or hybrid?

This single decision changes your cost by 40% or more. For most Indian homes with a reliable connection, the answer is on-grid.

If your area gets occasional short cuts, an on-grid system plus your existing inverter-battery for essential loads is almost always cheaper than a full hybrid. Only go hybrid if outages are long and frequent.
Decoding the spec sheet

Panel technology, explained without the jargon

Four terms cover almost everything a salesperson will say to you.

Polycrystalline

The older, cheaper technology — recognisable by its blue, speckled look. Efficiency around 16–17%, meaning you need more roof area for the same output. Still fine for off-grid and budget projects, but it has largely been replaced for rooftops.

Mono PERC

The current mainstream. Uniform black cells, 19–21% efficiency, and a strong price-to-performance balance. This is what most Indian homes are installing today, and what most DCR subsidy modules use.

N-Type TOPCon

The newer generation. Efficiency of 21–23%, noticeably better output on hot afternoons, slower annual degradation, and warranties reaching 30 years. Costs more per watt but often delivers cheaper electricity across 25 years.

Bifacial

Glass on both faces, so the panel also harvests light reflected from the surface beneath it. On a white or light-coloured terrace with an elevated structure this can add 5–15%. On a dark roof with panels mounted flush, it adds close to nothing.

Half-cut cells

Cells are cut in half, halving the current through each and so cutting resistive losses. The real benefit is shading: because the panel is wired as two independent halves, a shadow across the bottom row doesn't take out the whole panel. Worth having on Indian roofs, which are rarely shade-free.

The two warranties

Every panel has two. The product warranty (10–15 years) covers manufacturing defects — the panel physically failing. The power output warranty (25–30 years) guarantees the panel still produces a stated percentage of its rating, typically 80–87% at end of term. Salespeople quote the longer number. Ask for both.

Myth vs fact

Things people believe about solar that aren't true

Myth

"Solar doesn't work in the monsoon, so half the year is wasted."

Fact

Panels run on light, not direct sunshine, and still generate on overcast days — typically 30–50% of a clear-day figure. The 4 units per kW per day used in our calculator is an annual average that already accounts for the monsoon.

Myth

"Solar panels need constant expensive maintenance."

Fact

There are no moving parts. Rinsing the panels with plain water every few weeks in dusty season is the entire routine. The inverter may need attention once or twice across 25 years.

Myth

"My solar system will keep my house running during a power cut."

Fact

Not unless you have a hybrid system with a battery. A standard on-grid system deliberately shuts down during an outage — this is a safety requirement called anti-islanding, protecting linemen repairing the line.

Myth

"Panels will damage my roof or cause leaks."

Fact

A correctly engineered mounting structure spreads load and is properly sealed. Panels actually shade the slab beneath them, keeping the top floor measurably cooler. Bad installation causes leaks — solar does not.

Myth

"Higher wattage panels are always better."

Fact

What matters is total kW installed and the efficiency per square foot. A 550 W panel is only "better" than a 400 W one if your roof is tight. On a large roof, the cheaper panel per watt often wins.

Myth

"I should wait — prices will fall a lot more."

Fact

Module prices have already fallen dramatically and are now a minority of system cost. Meanwhile you keep paying rising tariffs every month you wait, and subsidy schemes have budgets and deadlines. Waiting has a real cost.

Myth

"A bigger system means a bigger subsidy."

Fact

The central subsidy caps at ₹78,000 for 3 kW. A 10 kW system receives exactly the same ₹78,000 as a 3 kW one. Anyone selling you extra capacity on subsidy grounds is misleading you.

Myth

"Solar panels stop working after 10 years."

Fact

They degrade slowly — typically about 0.5% a year. After 25 years a panel still produces around 80–87% of its original rating, which is why manufacturers guarantee exactly that in writing.

Looking after it

Maintenance is genuinely this simple

Do these five things and your system will outlive its warranty.

  • Rinse the panels every 2–4 weeks in dusty months, early morning or evening. Plain water and a soft brush. Never spray cold water on hot panels in the afternoon — thermal shock can crack glass.
  • Check your generation weekly on the inverter display or app. A sudden drop means a fault, a shaded panel or a dirty array. Catching it early is the whole game.
  • Trim growing trees before they start shading the array. A branch that shades one panel can pull down a whole string.
  • Inspect the structure once a year for rust, loose bolts and worn cable ties. Ten minutes of looking prevents the expensive failures.
  • Keep your documents — commissioning certificate, serial numbers, warranty cards, net metering agreement. You will need them for any claim.

When to call us instead

  • Generation drops more than 20% with no obvious cause
  • The inverter displays a fault code or stops exporting
  • You see discoloured cells, cracked glass, or burn marks on any panel
  • Any part of the structure has moved or a bolt has sheared
  • Your electricity bill rises unexpectedly despite normal generation — often a net meter issue

Never open the DC combiner box or touch the DC side yourself. Solar DC voltages are high and, unlike AC, cannot simply be switched off at a mains switch while the sun is up.

Glossary

Every term you'll hear, in plain English

Keep this open while you read any quotation.

kW is size — how much power the system can produce at once. kWh, called a "unit" on your bill, is quantity — power used over time. A 3 kW system running at full output for one hour makes 3 units.
Approved List of Models and Manufacturers. A government-maintained list of solar modules approved for use in subsidised and government projects. If your panel is not on it, your subsidy claim fails.
Domestic Content Requirement. Means both the solar cells and the finished module were manufactured in India. Mandatory for PM Surya Ghar residential subsidy.
Distribution Company — the utility that supplies your electricity and issues your bill. They approve your solar application, inspect the installation and fit the net meter.
The maximum power your connection is officially permitted to draw, printed on your bill in kW. Your solar system size generally cannot exceed it without applying for a load enhancement first.
A bi-directional meter that counts units flowing both ways — from the grid to you, and from your roof to the grid. Your bill is calculated on the difference.
Maximum Power Point Tracking. The inverter constantly adjusts to find the voltage at which your panels deliver the most power as light conditions change. Standard on all modern inverters.
A group of panels wired in series into one inverter input. Because they're in series, the weakest panel limits the string — which is why shading on one panel matters so much.
The safety feature that shuts your inverter down during a grid outage, so your system cannot energise a line that linemen believe is dead. It's why on-grid solar doesn't work in a power cut.
The slow annual decline in a panel's output, typically around 0.5% per year. The power warranty is the manufacturer's written guarantee about how far this can go.
Potential Induced Degradation. A failure mode where voltage stress causes lasting power loss. Quality modules are tested and marketed as "PID resistant" — worth checking on the datasheet.
How much output a panel loses per degree above 25°C, shown as something like −0.29%/°C. Closer to zero is better. It matters a great deal in Indian summers, and is where TOPCon beats Mono PERC.
Capacity Utilisation Factor is actual annual generation as a share of theoretical maximum — typically 16–19% for Indian rooftops. Performance Ratio compares actual output against what the panels should have produced given the sunlight received; above 75% is healthy.
